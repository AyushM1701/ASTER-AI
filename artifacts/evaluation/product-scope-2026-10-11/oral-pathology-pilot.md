[MED:42828028:full:0] Introduction

OSCC is one of the most common oral malignancies. Despite substantial advances in surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy, the overall prognosis of OSCC remains unsatisfactory, with local recurrence, cervical lymph node metastasis, and second primary tumors representing major factors affecting patients’ long-term survival ( Du et al., 2020 ; Wu et al., 2025 ; Global Burden of Disease, 2019 Cancer Collaboration et al., 2022 ; Bray et al., 2024 ; Lee et al., 2021 ; Tan et al., 2023 ). Compared with reactive treatment after the establishment of invasive carcinoma, identifying high-risk lesions and implementing effective interventions during the precancerous stage may provide greater potential to reduce OSCC incidence and mortality. Therefore, OPMDs provide a critical opportunity for both primary and secondary prevention of OSCC ( Warnakulasuriya et al., 2021 ).

[MED:42828028:full:1] Introduction

Conventional OPMD management primarily relies on clinical features and histopathological grading of epithelial dysplasia; however, this approach has substantial limitations ( Warnakulasuriya, 2019 ). Importantly, some lesions with mild dysplasia may undergo malignant transformation, whereas others with moderate-to-severe dysplasia may remain stable during long-term follow-up ( Chaturvedi et al., 2020 ; Pimenta-Barros et al., 2025 ). Moreover, substantial clinical, biological, and prognostic heterogeneity exists even within individual disease entities. Therefore, OPMDs should not be regarded simply as “oral precancerous plaques,” but rather as a heterogeneous disease spectrum shaped by diverse risk exposures, immune states, stromal microenvironments, genetic backgrounds, and microbial ecosystems.

[MED:42828028:full:2] Introduction

Recent studies have highlighted that OPMDs with similar clinical phenotypes may harbor substantially different molecular landscapes and malignant transformation potentials, underscoring the necessity of molecular-based risk stratification ( Li et al., 2021 ; Kumari et al., 2022 ). In this context, redefining the continuum of malignant transformation from OPMDs to OSCC and establishing multidimensional dynamic risk stratification systems have become major priorities in the management of oral potentially malignant disorders. Accordingly, this review adopts a disease-oriented comparative framework to examine major OPMDs across several interrelated dimensions, including clinical features and patterns of malignant transformation, major disease-specific mechanisms, molecular and microenvironmental alterations, potential biomarkers, and their relevance to risk stratification and clinical management. Rather than treating OPMDs as a homogeneous group of lesions, this framework aims to distinguish disease-specific progression pathways from shared mechanisms underlying malignant transformation and to link these differences to clinically relevant approaches to risk assessment and management (see Table 1 ).

[MED:42828028:full:3] Search strategy and selection criteria

A literature search was conducted in PubMed from March 25 to 23 May 2026, using the terms “oral leukoplakia” or “leukoplakia,” “proliferative verrucous leukoplakia,” “erythroplakia,” “oral lichen planus” or “lichen planus, oral,” “oral submucous fibrosis,” “oral potentially malignant disorders,” and “oral squamous cell carcinoma,” in combination with terms related to malignant transformation, molecular mechanisms, biomarkers, risk stratification, and treatment. Priority was given to studies published within the preceding 5 years. Earlier studies were also included when they provided important original evidence or when recent evidence was limited, particularly for oral erythroplakia. Studies were selected based on their relevance to malignant transformation, underlying mechanisms, biomarkers, risk stratification, and clinical management of oral potentially malignant disorders.

[MED:42828028:full:4] Oral leukoplakia

Oral leukoplakia (OLK) is the most common OPMD and exhibits marked clinical and molecular heterogeneity. The risk of malignant transformation is influenced by multiple factors, including sex, lesion location, size, multifocality, non-homogeneous or verrucous morphology, epithelial dysplasia, and exposure to carcinogenic factors such as tobacco, alcohol, and areca nut ( Pimenta-Barros et al., 2025 ; Zhu et al., 2021 ; Bhattarai et al., 2024 ). Lesions involving the lateral tongue, ventral tongue, and floor of the mouth are generally considered to carry a higher risk of malignant transformation ( Pimenta-Barros et al., 2025 ). A large retrospective cohort study using electronic health records reported an 8.4% malignant transformation rate for OLK, with a median time to malignant transformation of approximately 25 months; HIV-positive status was associated with an increased risk of malignant transformation ( Villa et al., 2025 ).

[MED:42828028:full:5] Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia

Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia (PVL) is recognized as a distinct oral potentially malignant disorder rather than a subtype of conventional oral leukoplakia ( Warnakulasuriya et al., 2021 ). PVL typically follows a persistent and progressive clinical course, initially presenting as one or more white keratotic lesions that gradually enlarge and extend to involve additional oral sites; with disease progression, the lesions may become verrucous or exophytic ( Warnakulasuriya et al., 2021 ). Because histopathologic alterations in early lesions may be subtle, establishing a definitive diagnosis of PVL requires longitudinal clinicopathologic assessment, particularly to determine whether the lesions become multifocal or show progressive enlargement ( Thompson et al., 2021 ).

[MED:42828028:full:6] Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia

PVL is characterized by an exceptionally high and sustained risk of malignant transformation. A meta-analysis of 22 studies involving 699 patients reported a malignant transformation rate of 45.8% over a mean follow-up of 7.2 years, with a mean interval of approximately 4.1 years from diagnosis to malignant transformation. Among patients who developed malignancy, 46.5% developed multiple primary oral cancers at distinct oral sites ( Palaia et al., 2021 ). PVL occurs predominantly in middle-aged and older individuals, with a higher prevalence among women, whereas traditional tobacco and alcohol exposure is relatively uncommon. The gingiva and alveolar ridge are commonly involved and also represent frequent sites of malignant transformation ( Palaia et al., 2021 ). Its progressive multifocal course, high recurrence rate, and propensity for multiple primary cancers collectively indicate that PVL represents a high-risk OPMD with distinctive clinical characteristics.

[MED:42828028:full:7] Oral erythroplakia

Oral erythroplakia (OE) is a relatively uncommon OPMD with a very high risk of malignant transformation, clinically presenting as persistent intensely red or velvety plaques that cannot be scraped off, with some lesions containing white keratotic components ( Das et al., 2022 ). The characteristic red appearance of OE is associated with epithelial atrophy, reduced keratinization, prominent visualization of vessels within the lamina propria, and inflammatory infiltration, and is frequently accompanied by marked cytological atypia and impaired epithelial maturation ( Shafer and Waldron, 1975 ). The histopathological spectrum of OE ranges from varying degrees of epithelial dysplasia and carcinoma in situ to early-stage squamous cell carcinoma; therefore, a high index of clinical suspicion is warranted upon initial detection ( Neville and Day, 2002 ). Studies have reported that the overall malignant transformation rate of OE ranges from 19.9% to 45%, with some large electronic health record cohorts reporting rates as high as 50.0%. The median time to malignant transformation is only 3.7 months, highlighting its rapid malignant evolution and high-risk nature ( Villa et al., 2025 ; Lorenzo-Pouso et al., 2022 ; Iocca et al., 2020 ; Villa et al., 2011 ; Öhman et al., 2023 ). Clinically, persistent unexplained red or red-and-white lesions should be biopsied promptly. When necessary, multiple-site sampling or complete excision should be considered, followed by long-term surveillance.

[MED:42828028:full:8] Oral lichen planus

Oral lichen planus (OLP) is a T cell-mediated chronic immune-inflammatory mucosal disorder, and its malignant transformation risk remains controversial. This controversy mainly arises from variations in diagnostic criteria, inconsistent distinction between OLP and oral lichenoid lesions (OLLs) as separate disease entities, differences in follow-up duration, and the presence of coexisting risk exposures ( Louisy et al., 2024 ). Recent studies suggest that the overall malignant transformation rate of OLP is approximately 1%–2%, whereas oral lichenoid lesions may exhibit a higher risk of malignant transformation ( González-Moles et al., 2019 ; Ramos-García et al., 2021 ; González-Moles et al., 2022 ). Large-scale genetic and epidemiological studies have demonstrated that, after excluding concomitant leukoplakia, patients with OLP still have significantly increased risks of tongue, gingival, and lip cancers, with an approximately 1.9% 10-year risk of oral cancer development ( Re et al., 2024 ). Therefore, the malignant transformation risk of OLP should not be overestimated; however, closer surveillance is recommended for patients with tongue involvement, erosive or atrophic forms, erythematous lesions, and persistent inflammatory activity.

[MED:42828028:full:9] Oral submucous fibrosis

Oral submucous fibrosis (OSMF) is strongly associated with areca nut chewing and represents a substantial health burden, particularly among populations in South Asia, Southeast Asia, and associated immigrant communities ( Reichart and Nguyen, 2008 ; Gupta and Jawanda, 2021 ; Cirillo et al., 2022 ; Tang et al., 2025 ). Its clinical manifestations include burning pain, mucosal pallor, fibrous band formation, and progressive restriction of mouth opening ( Warnakulasuriya et al., 2021 ; Tang et al., 2025 ). OSMF is driven by established carcinogenic exposure, chronic inflammation, tissue hypoxia, epithelial atrophy, and fibrotic matrix remodeling, and is therefore recognized as a high-risk OPMD ( Warnakulasuriya et al., 2021 ; Tang et al., 2025 ; Hu Z-Y. et al., 2026 ; Xiong et al., 2025 ). Cohort studies have reported an approximately 5.1% malignant transformation rate for OSMF, with a median time to malignant transformation of approximately 36 months. However, substantial variability exists among studies, which may be attributable to differences in population characteristics, diagnostic criteria, histopathological grading, and follow-up duration ( Villa et al., 2025 ).

[MED:42828028:full:10] Oral submucous fibrosis

Although OLK, PVL, OE, OLP, and OSMF differ markedly in their clinical manifestations and rates of malignant transformation, they share common biological features, including progressive disruption of epithelial homeostasis and remodeling of the surrounding microenvironment. Notably, malignant transformation of OPMDs does not follow a uniform linear pathway. Instead, distinct disease entities exhibit unique evolutionary patterns, in which chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, genetic and epigenetic alterations, immune dysregulation, epithelial-mesenchymal interactions, and microbiota dysbiosis contribute to varying extents. As shown in Figure 1 , OPMDs do not represent a single precancerous entity but rather a highly heterogeneous spectrum of disorders. Different diseases progress along disease-specific evolutionary trajectories and may ultimately develop into OSCC. This conceptual framework highlights the heterogeneity of OPMDs and provides a basis for understanding the diverse molecular mechanisms underlying their progression.

[MED:42828028:full:11] Oral leukoplakia: genomic instability, immunosuppression, and field cancerization effects

OLK represents one of the most representative OPMDs characterized by concurrent clinical and molecular heterogeneity. Histological assessment of epithelial dysplasia remains the most widely used parameter for risk evaluation; however, dysplasia grading is limited by substantial subjectivity and poor interobserver agreement ( Odell et al., 2021 ; Khoury et al., 2022 ). Accumulating evidence indicates that TP53 mutations, CDKN2A inactivation, EGFR overexpression, Cyclin D1 upregulation, activation of the PI3K/AKT/β-catenin pathway, and loss of heterozygosity at 3p, 9p, and 17p loci contribute to malignant transformation of OLK ( Wils et al., 2023 ; Normando et al., 2023 ; Ghosh et al., 2022 ; Monteiro et al., 2022 ; Jiang et al., 2001 ; Li et al., 2025a ; Cai et al., 2023 ). The burden of copy number alterations and specific genomic changes in leukoplakia tissues, including 3p14 deletion, 8p23 deletion, and 20p11 amplification, are associated with increased malignant transformation risk ( Wils et al., 2023 ; Ramos-García et al., 2022 ); TP53 mutations also represent relatively robust predictors of malignant progression.

[MED:42828028:full:12] Oral leukoplakia: genomic instability, immunosuppression, and field cancerization effects

Beyond genetic alterations within epithelial cells, dysregulation of the OLK immune microenvironment is increasingly recognized as an early event during malignant transformation. A single-cell RNA-sequencing study of human tissue identified IDO1-positive macrophages as a potential immunosuppressive cell population associated with the malignant transformation of oral leukoplakia. In vitro and in vivo experiments further indicated that these cells can be activated through the IFN-γ–JAK–STAT pathway and interact with exhausted T cells, thereby facilitating immune evasion ( Zhang Y. et al., 2024 ). Tobacco exposure may reprogram macrophage glutamine metabolism through the nicotine–α7nAChR axis, inducing M2 polarization and secretion of immunosuppressive mediators such as IL-10, thereby promoting dysregulated proliferation of oral epithelial cells ( Zhu et al., 2021 ). Moreover, emerging evidence implicates several regulatory networks, including the migrasome–PTGES–PGE2–SPP1-positive macrophage signaling cascade, macrophage–BMP5 axis, FAP–ITGB1–PI3K/AKT pathway, HOXC-AS1/EZH2/HOXC9 axis, and DIRAS2–RAF/MEK/MAPK pathway, as potential mechanisms and therapeutic targets in OLK malignant transformation ( Li et al., 2025a ; Jiang et al., 2026 ; Li et al., 2026 ; Chen et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025b ).

[MED:42828028:full:13] Oral leukoplakia: genomic instability, immunosuppression, and field cancerization effects

Field cancerization effects may explain why some patients experience recurrence, malignant transformation, or second primary tumors in adjacent or distant mucosal sites despite surgical excision of focal lesions. Low-depth whole-genome sequencing and morphology-associated copy number alteration (CNA) analysis have demonstrated that morphologically normal or low-grade dysplastic epithelial regions may already harbor aneuploidy or high-risk CNA clones, suggesting that molecular alterations may precede histopathological evidence of malignant transformation ( Li et al., 2021 ).

[MED:42828028:full:14] Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia: multifocal progression, immune dysregulation, and field cancerization

PVL appears to follow a pattern of malignant progression distinct from the conventional model of focal, stepwise progression typically associated with oral leukoplakia (OLK). Its persistent multifocality, continued emergence of new lesions at anatomically distinct sites, high recurrence rate after local treatment, and pronounced propensity for multiple primary cancers are consistent with a field cancerization phenotype ( Palaia et al., 2021 ). This conceptual framework provides a plausible explanation for why excision of a single lesion does not eliminate the subsequent risk of new lesions or malignant transformation elsewhere in the oral mucosa.

[MED:42828028:full:15] Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia: multifocal progression, immune dysregulation, and field cancerization

Immune remodeling may contribute to this biological behavior. PVL lesions exhibit increased infiltration of CD3 + and CD8 + T cells within the subepithelial compartment, with higher CD8 + T-cell density being associated with high-risk epithelial dysplasia ( Fernandes et al., 2023 ). However, concurrent alterations in cytokine profiles and T-cell subset imbalance suggest that prominent lymphocytic infiltration does not necessarily translate into effective antitumor immunity. In parallel, increased numbers of CD163 + and FXIIIa + macrophages, together with reduced numbers of mature dendritic cells, have been observed within PVL lesions, suggesting impaired antigen presentation and a shift toward a locally immunosuppressive microenvironment ( Palaçon et al., 2023 ).

[MED:42828028:full:16] Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia: multifocal progression, immune dysregulation, and field cancerization

Multi-omics studies provide further evidence that PVL is biologically distinct from conventional oral leukoplakia. Comparative transcriptomic analyses have identified distinct gene expression patterns involving altered keratinization and stromal and extracellular matrix (ECM)-related signatures, while genome-wide DNA methylation studies have revealed widespread DNA hypermethylation and proteomic analyses have identified disease-associated protein expression profiles ( Farah et al., 2024 ; Herreros-Pomares et al., 2021 ; Arroyo et al., 2023 ). Although these studies are limited by relatively small cohort sizes and require validation in independent longitudinal studies, their findings collectively suggest that PVL does not merely represent conventional OLK with a higher rate of malignant transformation, but rather a biologically distinct disease entity characterized by widespread epithelial alterations, persistent molecular remodeling, and dysregulated immune–stromal surveillance.

[MED:42828028:full:17] Oral erythroplakia: a high-risk phenotype driven by high-grade dysplasia

Compared with OLK, molecular studies of OE remain relatively limited; however, its clinicopathological risk profile is more clearly defined and concentrated. Erythroplakic mucosa commonly exhibits epithelial thinning, impaired maturation, marked cytological atypia, and vascular dilation, resulting in a clinical red appearance that frequently corresponds to high-grade pathological alterations ( Shafer and Waldron, 1975 ). OE can be regarded as a high-risk OPMD primarily driven by high-grade dysplasia, with malignant progression often occurring within a relatively short time frame.

This study employed the following substances and devices: aminolevulinic acid (Fudan Zhangjiang Bio-Pharmaceutical; Shanghai, China), thermosensitive gel (Fudan Zhangjiang Bio-Pharmaceutical), semiconductor laser treatment instrument set at a wavelength of 635 nm±5 nm (Yage Optoelectronic Technology; Wuhan, China), UV purple flashlight with a wavelength of 395 nm (Guangzhongdao Electronics; Shenzhen, China).

[MED:42808717:full:3] PDT Procedure

Preoperative photographs were taken, and the lesion area was measured using Photoshop software (Adobe; San Jose, CA, USA). 5-ALA powder (118 mg per vial) was dissolved in 0.5 ml of thermosensitive gel to obtain a 20% solution. An impression compound (Shanghai Rongxiang Dental Material; Shanghai, China) was softened in hot water (60° to 70°C).

[MED:42808717:full:4] PDT Procedure

Sterile cotton soaked with 20% 5-ALA was applied to the oral leukoplakia, fully covering the lesion and extending 3 to 5 mm beyond its margins. The dressing was secured with 4 to 5 layers of glutinous rice paper and then covered with plastic wrap (Fig 2a).

[MED:42808717:full:5] PDT Procedure

For fixation, the softened impression compound was molded according to the lesion site (Fig 2b). For ventral tongue lesions, an oval shape was made (thickness 5 to 10 mm, width 10 to 15 mm beyond the lesion borders). It was then placed flat over the wrapped dressing, without exceeding the inner border of the teeth. After cooling, the compound hardened, using the gap between the teeth and tongue to maintain stable contact and prevent displacement (Fig 2c). For gingival lesions, an elongated block (same dimensions) was placed over the dressing and extended over the occlusal surfaces. The patient was asked to bite down gently, securing the compound in the space between the teeth and cheek (Fig 2d). To manage salivary contamination, the patient’s head was tilted slightly to the contralateral side, and a saliva ejector was placed at the contralateral mouth angle to remove flowing saliva. The total application time was 3 h; after 1.5 h, the photosensitizer-soaked cotton was replaced, and the fixation process was repeated to ensure consistent tissue contact throughout.

[MED:42808717:full:6] PDT Procedure

After 3 h, the dressing was removed, and the lesion was irradiated perpendicularly with a UV flashlight. Red fluorescence indicated good photosensitizer absorption (Fig 2e). Conversely, the absence of red fluorescence was considered to indicate poor photosensitizer absorption, and patients in which fluorescence was lacking were excluded. 16

[MED:42808717:full:7] PDT Procedure

Under local anesthesia, the lesion was illuminated with a laser (wavelength 635 nm, energy density 100 J/cm 2 ) for 20 min. Specifically, the irradiation was carried out in cycles of 5-min illumination, with 3-min intervals between each cycle (Fig 2f).

[MED:42808717:full:8] Follow-up

Treatment was conducted every 7 to 10 days, with three sessions constituting one course of treatment. At each follow-up session, the lesion areas were measured. According to the condition of the lesion, each patient underwent 1 or 2 treatment courses. If there were no apparent OL lesions post-treatment, the treatment could be temporarily halted, with efficacy evaluated at the first month post-treatment. If the change in lesion area was less than 30% after three consecutive 5-ALA-PDT treatments, the treatment was discontinued. Patients were re-examined monthly for the first six months post-surgery and every three months thereafter.

[MED:42808717:full:9] Clinical Efficacy valuation

Efficacy was assessed one month after the completion of treatment. According to the Expert Consensus on 5-Aminolevulinic Acid Photodynamic Therapy for Oral Potentially Malignant Disorder, 14 the clinical evaluation criteria for treatment response were as follows: complete remission (CR), defined as complete disappearance of the treated lesion; partial response (PR), defined as at least a 30% reduction in lesion size compared with baseline; stable disease (SD), defined as no change in lesion size or type; and progressive disease (PD), defined as the appearance of new lesions or an increase in lesion area of at least 20% compared with baseline.

[MED:42808717:full:10] Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM; Armonk, NY, USA). Measurement data conforming to normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed measurement data or rank data were described using the median M (Q25, Q75). Countable data were described using frequency and percentage. For within-group comparisons, the paired rank-sum test was used. For measurement data, parametric (independent samples t-test, ANOVA) or non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis H-test) were used to compare the number of treatments between different groups. The chi-squared test, corrected chi-squared, and Fisher’s exact test were used to compare differences between groups. A p-value \< 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

[MED:42808717:full:12] Clinical Efficacy of 5-ALA-PDT

Before treatment, the range of OL lesions was 0.52 cm 2 to 5.43 cm 2 , with a median of 2.14 cm 2 . After treatment, the range was 0 cm 2 to 3.68 cm 2 , with a median of 0.15 cm 2 . [MED:42808717:full:17] DISCUSSION

Although 5-ALA-PDT has been used to treat OL for over a decade, detailed reports on its technical procedures remain scarce. Moreover, variations in treatment protocols and parameters among different studies have led to considerable differences in the specific procedures employed by physicians and, consequently, in clinical treatment outcomes. Firstly, this study utilized photoshop software to measure the lesion area, demonstrating that it is more accurate than traditional periodontal probes in reflecting changes in lesion size, thus facilitating data collection and analysis. Secondly, building upon the 5-ALA-PDT treatment guidelines for OL established by Chen et al, 6 this study further optimized the treatment process to enhance the clinical efficacy of 5-ALA-PDT for OL. Glutinous rice paper, due to its safety and edibility, has been widely used in previous studies for fixing photosensitizer dressings. 8 , 18 Our preliminary research found that initial fixation with glutinous rice paper and gauze was effective.

[MED:41988349:full:0] Introduction

Oral leukoplakia (OL) is defined by the World Health Organization as a predominantly white plaque of questionable risk having excluded other known diseases or disorders that carry no increased risk for cancer ( 1 ). OL is classified within the broader group of oral potentially malignant disorders (OPMDs), which encompass a spectrum of clinically and histopathologically diverse lesions with varying risks of malignant transformation. With a prevalence of 1.39% worldwide, the most common type of OPMD is oral leukoplakia ( 2 ). To date, the natural history of OL progression is poorly understood. This is the focus of a large area of research with the aim to better stratify risk in oral lesions. The grade of dysplasia as mild, moderate or severe, is currently used to assess risk of malignant transformation but is not completely predictive ( 3 ). Additional biomarkers are sought to aid in identifying lesions with high risk for malignant transformation. The immune system is known to play a role in the progression of OL. Recent single cell RNA sequencing data of progressing oral dysplastic lesions show expansion of specific immune cells thought to contribute to inhibition of the innate immune surveillance ( 4 ). Several myeloid cell subclusters were found to be expanded in this process, including dendritic cells that express HLA-DR ( 5 ). Presence of HLA-DR is known to correlate with increased risk of autoimmune diseases. Less is known about it, or other associated differential HLA expression and oral cancer risk.

[MED:41988349:full:1] Introduction

The evasion of immune surveillance plays a key role in oncogenesis. Normal immune surveillance involves antigen presentation by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules to T cells to initiate immune responses. Dysregulation of this process is thought to contribute to development of cancer ( 6 ). HLA is comprised of classical class I and class II molecules, and non-classical molecules such as HLA-E and G. HLA Class I molecules (HLA-A,B,C) are primarily involved in presenting various antigens such as viral proteins or mutated proteins in cancerous cells to CD8+ cytotoxic T cells for destruction. HLA Class II molecules (DR, DQ, DP) present antigens to CD4+ helper T cells, which play a critical role in coordinating the immune response. Non-classical HLA-E and HLA-G are involved in maintaining immune tolerance and modulating immune responses in specific contexts by interacting with specific inhibitory receptors found on NK cells, T cells, and other immune cells. By binding these receptors, HLA-E and HLA-G inhibit immune responses ( 7 ). The dysregulation of HLA expression has been reported in cancer and precancerous conditions ( 8 ). For example, overexpression or dysregulation of non-classical HLA molecules such as HLA-G and HLA-E has been correlated with the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment and thus immune evasion ( 9 , 10 ). This could represent a potential marker for a developing malignancy.

[MED:41988349:full:2] Introduction

Immunologic changes in the microenvironment of oral leukoplakia are thought to precede malignant transformation ( 8 , 9 ). RNA sequencing studies show expansion of immune suppressive T cells and other inhibitory clusters that create a permissive environment for unchecked epithelial cell proliferation ( 11 , 12 ). Additional studies show mechanisms of T cell sequestration away from sites of tumor formation, functionally impairing their ability to detect and kill oral precancer and cancerous cells ( 13 ). It is difficult to quantify and use individual cell types or populations as biomarkers given challenges in quantifying and comparing their absolute amounts across slides and patients. It is thought that certain types of HLA expression, or loss of normal Class I MHC molecules may confer risk of immune dysregulation and thus cancer. Studies have shown that increased expression of non-classical HLA molecules, such as HLA-G and HLA-E, are present in oral leukoplakia. Moreover, research of HLA-G and HLA-E shows inhibition of cytotoxic T cell and Natural Killer (NK) cell activity, potentially suppressing anti-tumor immunity ( 14 , 15 ). HLA-G and HLA-E are also associated with the secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, such as IL-10 and IFN-γ, which are known to potentiate immune evasion in oral leukoplakia ( 9 , 10 ). This suggests that nonclassical HLA may play a role in progressing oral leukoplakia to malignancy.

[MED:41988349:full:3] Introduction

For this reason, we have completed a scoping literature review to further examine the role HLA may play in the malignant transformation of oral leukoplakia. We studied manuscripts that describe oral leukoplakia/premalignant lesions and risk of oral cancer development based upon HLA expression. The main exposure being investigated is expression of HLA subtypes and risk of lesion progression to oral cancer. The comparison is different HLA subtypes (Class I vs. Class II vs. non classical HLA subtype) expressed in oral premalignant lesions to assess the outcome of malignant transformation of oral leukoplakia.

[MED:41988349:full:4] Methods

We conducted a scoping review following the PRISMA (preferred reporting items of systematic reviews and meta-analyses) guidelines as shown in Figure 1 . We included all studies on oral leukoplakia and HLA from 1989 to 2025. The quality of studies was evaluated based on their type and relevance to the topic. No study type was excluded or prioritized. The review was conducted across three databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science. Search strategy for PubMed (MEDLINE) was \[“Leukoplakia, Oral”(Mesh) OR leukoplakia OR pre-malignant OR pre-cancerous\] AND \[“Interleukin-10”(Mesh) OR CD8 OR CD 57 OR CD68 OR HLA-G OR HLA-E OR CD68 OR IL-10\], and for EMBASE and web of science (“Leukoplakia, Oral” OR leukoplakia OR pre-malignant OR pre-cancerous) AND (“Interleukin-10” OR CD8 OR CD 57 OR CD68 OR HLA-G OR HLA-E OR CD68 OR IL-10). Exclusion criteria were as follows: animal studies, Epstein–Barr Virus, Lupus erythematosus, Human Papillomavirus, oral psoriasis, congenital dyskeratosis, Human immunodeficiency virus, and tobacco. Tobacco exposure was excluded to reduce confounding, as tobacco-associated leukoplakia demonstrates distinct molecular, inflammatory, and immunologic profiles compared to idiopathic OL. Exclusion of tobacco-related lesions allowed for a more focused assessment of HLA-mediated immune mechanisms independent of a well-established carcinogenic exposure. Studies were screened by title to assess for retrieval ( n = 15), then full abstracts were read to determine final eligibility ( n = 5). Studies were excluded by population (see exclusion criteria), and condition of interest (premalignant vs. malignant oral lesions).

[MED:41988349:full:5] Results

Upon applying the search criteria to PubMed, EMBASE, and Web of Science, 293 studies were reviewed on Covidence. After using the appropriate exclusion criteria, five studies were evaluated for the purpose of this scoping review. The five studies included in this scoping review varied in study design, sample size, tissue source, and analytic methodology, but all evaluated the relationship between HLA-related markers and oral leukoplakia or its progression toward malignancy ( Table 1 ).

[MED:41988349:full:6] Results

Goncalves et al. performed immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis for HLA-E and HLA-G in OL, and staining was noted in both cytoplasm and cell membranes ( Table 1 ). Patients with OL underwent an incisional biopsy between 2011 and 2016, and a total of 80 samples were collected. The control group ( n = 20) consisted of saliva from healthy individuals and tissue from clinically and histologically normal oral mucosa obtained from biopsies of gingival mucosa. Two oral pathologists independently graded epithelial dysplasia based on the criteria described by the WHO and by the Kujan et al. binary system (high/low-risk of malignant transformation). Samples showed the following distribution: severe dysplasia ( n = 15), moderate ( n = 20), mild ( n = 32), and no dysplasia ( n = 13), with 18 samples with high-risk and 62 samples with low-risk of malignant transformation.

[MED:41988349:full:7] Results

HLA- G and E were evaluated in epithelial cells (keratinocytes) and adjacent connective tissue cells (immune-inflammatory, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts). Strength of staining was assessed by calculating an immunoreactive score. Of note, lesions that had higher expressions of HLA-E and HLA-G by IHC had a higher incidence of malignant transformation compared to the controls. This finding was independent of the degree of dysplasia. An additional significant finding is that the expression of HLA-G and E in oral leukoplakia correlated with the levels of expression seen in the OSCC group ( 8 ). The study is limited by its retrospective design and lack of longitudinal follow-up, which prevents direct evaluation of malignant transformation over time. Additionally, while the sample size was relatively large compared with other studies in this review, the use of immunohistochemical scoring introduces potential observer variability despite the use of standardized scoring methods.

[MED:41988349:full:8] Results

Similarly, Fregonezi et al. collected oral biopsies (benign, oral leukoplakia, and oral squamous cell carcinomas) and analyzed them for anti-HLA-G antibodies ( 16 ). 51 formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded oral biopsies from archives between 1992 and 2005 were used. The biopsies were stratified into three groups as follows: oral benign lesions (oral hyperplasia and papilloma, n = 16), oral premalignant lesions (oral leukoplakia with dysplasia and lichen planus, n = 17), and malignant lesions (oral squamous cell carcinoma, n = 18). Tumor grades were evaluated histologically and IHC was performed using the streptavidin-biotin system for the detection of HLA-G antigens ( Table 1 ). The results were classified as negative when the cytoplasm was immunolabelling (≤25%), and positive (immunolabeling \<25% until 100%). The positive control was a section of trophoblastic tissue from a third-trimester human placenta, while the negative control was the same human tissue used as positive control, in which the primary antibody was omitted from the assay. High levels of HLA-G (30.6 ± 5.274) were found in benign and (18.6 ± 7.189) premalignant oral lesions. In contrast, low levels of HLA-G (20.51 ± 5.555) were observed in malignant oral lesions. Statistical significance was observed between high expression of HLA-G in benign vs. premalignant and malignant oral lesions ( p \< 0.05). Interpretation of these findings is limited by the relatively small sample size and the grouping of different premalignant conditions within the same category. Additionally, the cross-sectional design and lack of long-term follow-up prevents determination of whether lesions with higher HLA-G expression ultimately progressed to carcinoma.

[MED:41988349:full:9] Results

To investigate the potential role circRNAs might in the pathogenesis of OL and Oral lichen planus (OLP), Yang et al. examined their differential expression as compared to normal and cancerous tissues ( Table 1 ). They utilized high throughput sequencing technology to determine which circRNAs were enriched in these diseases as compared to normal tissue and confirmed the findings through PCR analysis. Five circRNAs were highly expressed in OL, of which circHLA-C was found to have the most elevated expression on sequencing. In addition, gene ontology functional analysis showed increased activity of biologic pathways associated with immune processes, indicating its potential marker of OL development, and its distinction from normal mucosa and possible marker of early detection for oral cancer ( 17 ). However, the study relied primarily on sequencing-based discovery methods, limiting conclusions regarding the clinical applicability of circHLA-C as a biomarker.

[MED:41988349:full:10] Results

In a similar study, Xu et al. studied six OL tissue samples were obtained from two male and four female patients, compared to 6 samples or normal oral mucosa from 2018 ( Table 1 ). Based on the diagnostic criteria of WHO, the patients were diagnosed by histopathological examination. Xu et al. revealed that the most upregulated circRNA in oral leukoplakia was circHLA-C ( 18 ). The diagnostic potential of circHLA-C was evaluated using qRT-PCR, yielding an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.955. A statistically significant correlation was observed between circHLA-C expression levels and the degree of dysplasia, suggesting its potential role as a biomarker for oral leukoplakia progression ( 18 ). Despite these promising findings, the small sample size and study design significantly limit the generalizability of the results, highlighting the need for larger studies to confirm the diagnostic and prognostic value of circHLA-C.

[MED:41988349:full:11] Results

Lastly, Bondad-Palmario used 100 samples of oral leukoplakia histologically graded based on WHO criteria. Immunohistochemical controls consisted of the replacement of primary antibodies with normal or blocking serum. All specimens that reacted with HLA-DR antibody gave positive results, which were especially strong in Langerhans cells. As the expression of HLA-DR was widely distributed on numerous different cell types, the cell count was not determined. Bondad-Palmario did note that HLA-DR expression was increased in keratinocytes in severely dysplastic oral leukoplakia lesions ( 19 ). However, because HLA-DR is widely expressed among various immune and epithelial cell types, quantitative cell counting and analysis was not performed, limiting the ability to compare expression levels between lesions. In addition, the study lacked a control group of normal mucosa or long-term clinical follow-up, making it difficult to determine whether increased HLA-DR expression is predictive of malignant transformation or simply reflective of local immune activation.

[MED:41988349:full:12] Results

Collectively, these studies suggest that alterations in HLA-related molecules and associated regulatory pathways may contribute to immune modulation in oral leukoplakia and its potential progression toward malignancy. However, the existing literature is limited by sample size, heterogeneous methodologies, and retrospective or cross-sectional designs. The lack of longitudinal studies evaluating the predictive value of these markers for malignant transformation represents a significant gap in the current evidence base. Future research incorporating larger cohorts and prospective follow-up will be necessary to determine whether HLA-related markers can serve as reliable biomarkers for early detection or risk stratification in oral leukoplakia.

[MED:41988349:full:13] Discussion

Evasion of the host immune system is regarded as a hallmark of cancer development. It is thought that HLA may play a role in tumor immune response via antigen presentation and activation of innate and adaptive immunity for cancer cell destruction, and its dysfunction may permit epithelial cell proliferation ( 20 ). As so, the potential role of HLA expression in oncogenesis remains a topic of interest and was the reason for this investigation. In this review, Goncalves and Fregonezi et al. showed that HLA-G expression was increased in OL. Goncalves et al. found the same to be true for HLA-E, which was not studied by Fregonezi's group. In addition, Goncalves et al. found increased HLA-E and G expression to predict malignant transformation, independent of the degree of histologic dysplasia. Investigation into HLA risks of OL by Bondard-Palmario found increased HLA-DR expression to be present in severe dysplasia. Yang et al. and Xu et al. found that circHLA-C expression is upregulated in oral leukoplakia compared to normal mucosa, and correlates to dysplasia severity ( 17 , 18 ). This is corroborated by Momen-Heravi et al.'s work implicating circHLA-C in oral cancer initiation and progression. They detail the function of circHLA-C in oral cancer and as it relates to potential use as a diagnostic tool and target for therapy ( 21 ). Thus, in this literature review we found that increased HLA-E, G, circHLA-C, and DR expression was present in progressing OL. This furthers the idea that expression of non-classical HLA molecules in premalignant lesions such as OL may permit evasion of immune surveillance as an early event in oncogenesis and could serve as a biomarker for progression.

[MED:41988349:full:14] Discussion

Recently, circRNA has become a therapeutic target of interest for cancers of other types and autoimmune diseases ( 22 ). Loss-of-function approaches include the use of RNA interference (RNAi) mechanisms ( 23 ). These short dsRNA molecules target and bind the circRNA, silencing their effect and marking them for cleavage and destruction ( 24 ). Challenges with (RNAi) include their rapid degradation, difficulty with delivery and side effects. These studies provide intrigue into the potential future targetability of circRNAs in diseases which overexpress them, such as that of OL and the overexpression of circ-HLA-C.

[MED:41988349:full:15] Discussion

Inherent to the success of immune-based therapies for oral cancer and precancer is the implication of its role in cancer initiation and progression ( 25 , 26 ). While research examines the cellular landscape of this immune contribution, less investigation has been completed to detect the systems and cellular programs that regulate these cells ( 5 , 9 , 27 ). In addition, it is well recognized that multiple different immunologic phenotypes of OL exist, which complicates prognosticating lesion progression based upon cell types alone ( 28 – 30 ). This is the impetus for our investigation; to research a gap in the literature regarding what master regulators might exist in the host immune system that causes susceptibility to OL progression and malignant transformation. Given the known risk and susceptibility that expression of certain HLA molecules portends on autoimmune diseases, we endeavored on this investigation in oral cancer, despite and because of the paucity in literature. The limited number of studies and data to review in our investigation provides us the ability to describe our findings, but statistical analysis is not possible. This is the most important limitation to this review; however, we see it justified given the critical gap in our understanding of immunologic control mechanisms that lead to oral cancer. Additional limitations of our review include the retrospective nature of the investigations, with inherent recall and selection bias. In addition there was significant heterogeneity in study design and methods which make it difficult to draw conclusions.

[MED:41988349:full:16] Discussion

However, this review furthers the concept of immune mediated progression and transformation of oral premalignant lesions such as OL to oral cancer. Additional profiling of both classical and non-classical HLA molecules in oral leukoplakia may provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying the progression of oral leukoplakia to oral squamous cell carcinoma. HLA-G and HLA-E may function as biomarkers for high-risk oral leukoplakia exhibiting immune evasion. CircHLA-C may serve as both a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for OL. This review should serve as the basis for additional prospective investigation into potential diagnostic and therapeutic applications of HLA for oral leukoplakia and cancer.
